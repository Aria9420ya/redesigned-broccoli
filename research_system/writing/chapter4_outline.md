## Chapter Thesis

Electrospinning provides a structural route to engineer continuous, mechanically robust, and high-interfacial-area proton-conducting architectures, while COFs provide ordered pores, tunable acid/base sites, and confined proton carriers. For high-temperature PEMs, the central question is whether these two advantages can be integrated into membranes that retain phosphoric acid/ionic carriers, maintain dimensional stability, and deliver proton conduction under low-humidity or anhydrous conditions.

## 4.1 Why Electrospinning Matters For High-Temperature PEMs

Purpose:

Introduce electrospinning as a membrane architecture tool rather than a generic fabrication method.

Key points:

- High-temperature PEMs need proton transport under low water activity, acid retention, dimensional stability, and mechanical integrity.
- Electrospun nanofibers can form interconnected scaffolds, increase interfacial area, create anisotropic pathways, and reinforce polymer membranes.
- Nanofiber networks can host or anchor COFs, ionic liquids, phosphoric acid, or sulfonated domains.
- The benefit is only meaningful if the fiber network supports proton continuity rather than simply diluting ion-conducting polymer.

## 4.2 Structural Relationships Between COFs And Electrospun/Nanofiber Membranes

Purpose:

Classify how COFs are positioned relative to nanofibers. This is clearer than grouping only by preparation method.

Subsections:

### 4.2.1 Fiber-embedded architecture

Focus:

- COF is integrated into the electrospun fiber system during or after fiber formation.
- The fiber itself becomes a composite proton-conducting unit.
- Example: COF-doped PVDF nanofibers for high-temperature PEMs.

What to evaluate:

- COF loading and dispersion.
- Whether COF is inside fibers or exposed at interfaces.
- Ionic liquid/phosphoric acid retention.
- Fiber continuity and membrane densification.

Evidence needed:

- Fiber SEM/TEM.
- FTIR/XPS/PXRD confirming COF.
- Leaching or stability data.
- Conductivity versus temperature.

### 4.2.2 Surface-growth architecture

Focus:

- COF grown on PAN or other nanofiber surfaces.
- Stronger interfacial contact and less aggregation than physical blending.
- The fiber mainly acts as scaffold; COF acts as surface functional layer or interfacial proton-channel layer.

Core literature:

- In situ COF on PAN nanofibers/PFSA, 2022.

What to evaluate:

- COF crystallinity after growth.
- Surface coverage and pore accessibility.
- Through-plane proton continuity.
- Swelling and mechanical stability.

### 4.2.3 Network-bridged architecture

Focus:

- COF/CONs and nanofibers jointly form cross-scale connected networks.
- The emphasis is membrane-level connectivity rather than COF placement inside a single fiber.

Core literature:

- Study on hybrid COF connected 3D nanofiber structures in PEMs, 2024.
- Tuning sulfonated crystalline nanochannels in aramid nanofiber-reinforced CON membranes, 2026.
- Cellulose nanofiber-connected COF nanosheets composite membrane, 2024.

What to evaluate:

- Whether "3D network" is structural evidence or only conceptual language.
- Fiber-fiber junction chemistry.
- Proton-pathway continuity.
- Mechanical reinforcement.

## 4.3 Proton Transport Under High-Temperature Or Low-Humidity Conditions

Purpose:

Connect electrospun/COF architecture to high-temperature proton conduction mechanisms.

Key mechanisms:

- Phosphoric-acid assisted vehicular and hopping transport.
- Ionic-liquid mediated proton transport.
- Fixed sulfonic acid/proton donor sites in COF channels.
- Acid-base pairs and H-bond acceptors inside COF pores.
- Confined acid/water retention under low RH.

## 4.4 Performance Retention And Device Translation: From Stability To MEA

Purpose:

Discuss why high-temperature PEMs cannot be judged only by conductivity, and how electrospun COF architectures address stability and device-transfer problems.

Failure modes:

- Phosphoric acid leakage.
- Membrane swelling or dimensional instability.
- COF aggregation.
- Fiber/polymer interfacial defects.
- Mechanical brittleness.
- High ex situ conductivity but poor MEA performance.

Design principles:

- Use electrospun scaffolds for reinforcement and dimensional control.
- Use COF pores or ionic channels to confine acid carriers.
- Use in situ growth or compatible interfaces to reduce aggregation.
- Use flexible or rigid-flexible COF/polymer combinations to balance conductivity and toughness.
- Verify durability under heat, low RH, soaking, and fuel-cell operation.

Core literature:

- A1/A2 electrospun COF papers.
- P-COF/Nafion acid immobilization paper.
- SCOF/PTFE frontier benchmark.
- Rigid-flexible COF composite membrane, 2026.
- COF/PBI high-temperature papers.

Device-translation points:

- Ex situ conductivity is necessary but insufficient.
- MEA performance depends on membrane thickness, electrode contact, acid distribution, gas crossover, and interfacial resistance.
- High-temperature PEMs require long-term stability and acid retention, not just initial conductivity.
- Papers without MEA should be framed as material-level evidence.

Evidence hierarchy:

1. Verified material structure.
2. Conductivity with RH/T/thickness/direction.
3. Swelling, WU, IEC, mechanical data.
4. Acid retention/leaching and durability.
5. MEA performance under stated gas/RH/T/catalyst loading.

## 4.5 Design Rules For Engineering COFs Via Electrospinning

Purpose:

End the chapter with synthesis rather than a list of papers.

Design rules:

1. Place COFs where they contribute to continuous proton pathways, not merely as fillers.
2. Match COF chemistry to high-temperature carrier: sulfonic acid, phosphoric acid, ionic liquid, imidazole/triazole, or acid-base pairs.
3. Use electrospun scaffolds to control mechanical stability and swelling, but verify that they do not block through-plane conduction.
4. Prefer in situ growth, surface anchoring, or compatible interfaces over simple physical blending when aggregation is a risk.
5. Judge high-temperature PEMs by conductivity plus acid retention, durability, and MEA translation.

Closing sentence idea:

Electrospinning should be viewed not only as a membrane-shaping method but as a pathway-engineering tool that determines whether COF porosity can be translated into continuous, stable, and device-relevant proton transport.
